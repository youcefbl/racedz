Many runners turn every session into a moderately hard effort. That can make recovery and consistency difficult. A more sustainable approach is to **keep most runs genuinely easy**, using heart rate and perceived effort as guides rather than absolute rules.

## The five zones

Many apps use five zones based on a percentage of estimated maximum heart rate. The familiar **220 minus age** formula is only a rough population-level guide; individual maximums can differ substantially. Medication, health conditions, heat, fatigue, and sensor error can also change the reading. Use the zones below as one model, not a diagnosis.

| Zone | % of max HR | Feels like | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Zone 1 | 50–60% | Very easy walk/jog | Warm-up, recovery |
| **Zone 2** | **60–70%** | **Easy, can chat** | **Aerobic base — the big one** |
| Zone 3 | 70–80% | "Comfortably hard" | Steady/tempo |
| Zone 4 | 80–90% | Hard, short sentences | Threshold, speed |
| Zone 5 | 90–100% | All-out | Sprints, VO₂ max |

<Callout type="tip" title="No heart-rate monitor? Use the talk test">
Being able to speak in complete sentences is a useful check for an easy aerobic effort. It does not map perfectly to a numbered watch zone, but if you are gasping between words, easing off is sensible.
</Callout>

## Why easy aerobic running helps

Zone 2 feels *too* easy, which is exactly why most runners skip it. But easy aerobic running is what builds the engine:

- It grows the tiny blood vessels and mitochondria that let your muscles use oxygen efficiently.
- It teaches your body to burn fat for fuel, sparing your limited carbohydrate stores.
- It usually creates less fatigue than hard running, which may make consistent training easier to sustain.

Over time, you may be able to run the **same easy pace at a lower heart rate**, although heat, hydration, sleep, and sensor accuracy can affect day-to-day numbers. Trends across comparable runs matter more than one reading.

## Mostly easy, some hard

An often-cited observation is that endurance athletes complete a large majority of training at low intensity, sometimes summarized as **80% easy and 20% hard**. That ratio is not a universal prescription. Research in recreational runners found improvement with both polarized and more focused programs, without a clear statistically significant winner.

<Callout type="warning" title="The most common mistake">
Running easy days harder than planned can make recovery difficult. Keep them comfortable enough that you are ready for the sessions intended to be hard.
</Callout>

## How to start this week

1. Pick your **easy runs** and deliberately slow down until you can chat comfortably. It will feel strange — trust it.
2. If you are new, begin with **no more than one harder session** a week. More experienced runners may tolerate a second, with recovery between them.
3. Review several weeks of comparable runs rather than expecting a fixed change by a deadline.

Easy running is not a shortcut, but it can be a durable foundation. Stop and seek medical advice if exercise brings chest pain, fainting, unusual breathlessness, or other concerning symptoms.
