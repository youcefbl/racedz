Cadence is simply the number of steps you take per minute, counting both feet. Many watches display it, and many runners have heard that **180 steps per minute** is the correct number. It is not a universal rule. Your natural cadence changes with speed, body dimensions, terrain, fatigue, and running experience.

<Callout type="tip" title="The useful question">
Do not ask, “How do I reach 180?” Ask, “Is my current step rate comfortable and appropriate for this pace—and is there a reason to change it?”
</Callout>

## How to measure cadence

Use one of these methods during a steady, flat section of an easy run:

1. **Watch:** read the cadence field, but review an average over a few minutes rather than one jumpy instant.
2. **Count both feet:** count every foot contact for 30 seconds and multiply by 2.
3. **Count one foot:** count only the right or left foot for 30 seconds and multiply by 4.

Measure at a known pace. Comparing an easy jog with a fast interval tells you little because cadence normally rises as speed increases.

## Why 180 is not a pass-or-fail test

A single target ignores individual differences. Two runners at the same pace can use different combinations of step length and step rate. A lower number is not automatically bad, and a higher number is not automatically efficient.

The research is more nuanced: increasing cadence at a fixed speed often shortens the step and changes forces at the hip, knee, ankle, or foot. Systematic reviews find reasonably consistent biomechanical changes, but the evidence is still insufficient to say that changing cadence prevents injuries or improves performance for every runner.

## When a small change may help

A clinician may use step-rate retraining for a runner who overstrides, has a specific loading problem, or is returning from certain injuries. Studies commonly test increases of about 5–10% above a runner's own preferred cadence—not a jump to one universal number.

For example, if your normal cadence at a given easy pace is 164 steps per minute:

- A 3% increase is about 169.
- A 5% increase is about 172.
- Jumping straight to 180 is almost a 10% change.

Even a small change can feel awkward or raise effort at first. That is why a reason, a gradual progression, and feedback matter.

## A safe practice drill

If you have a clear reason to experiment and you are currently pain-free:

1. Warm up easily for 10 minutes.
2. Record your natural cadence at a steady pace.
3. Set a metronome or playlist around 3–5% higher.
4. Run three to five 1-minute bouts at that rhythm, with easy running between them.
5. Think “short, quiet steps,” not “run faster.” Keep the overall pace similar.

Try this once or twice a week before lengthening the bouts. Stop if the change creates pain, persistent calf or Achilles tightness, or a forced running pattern.

<Callout type="warning" title="Do not rebuild your stride from a watch number alone">
Cadence is one measurement, not a diagnosis. Persistent pain or a major gait change deserves assessment by a qualified sports-health professional rather than trial-and-error based on social media advice.
</Callout>

## What matters more than the perfect number

Comfort, consistency, gradual training, and the ability to hold form as you tire matter more than matching somebody else's cadence. Track your number if it helps you understand your running, but treat it as context—not a score.
